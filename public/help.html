Astrometric Scale Engine Interactive Relativistic Spaceflight Calculator — User Guide

Pick where you are leaving from and where you are going, pick an engine, and see how long the trip takes for you and for everyone left behind. Then press a button and watch the flight happen on a 3D map.

Overview

The Astrometric Scale Engine answers one question: how long does it really take to get there? It works for a hop to the Moon, a voyage to another star, or a trip to another galaxy. The live version is at astro.brucedgates.com.

Sublight engines use the real equations of special relativity. The warp drive uses a simple model of the Alcubierre warp bubble. Everything updates the moment you change a place, an engine or a slider.

Sublight Physics

True relativistic travel: steady acceleration felt by the crew, an automatic coast if the speed limit is reached, and a mirror-image braking burn. Ship time and Earth time are computed separately.

Warp Drive

A hypothetical Alcubierre bubble. The ship sits in flat space-time, so there is no time dilation and the clocks agree. No one knows how to build one.

Real Stellar Data

Stars come from the HYG star database, exoplanets from the NASA Exoplanet Archive, and deep-sky objects from the Messier catalog. Stars are drawn in their real colour and brightness.

Real Solar System

Planets, moons, dwarf planets, asteroids and spacecraft sit at real positions from JPL Horizons, measured in astronomical units (AU).

Quick Start

The page opens on a flight that is already planned: Earth to Mars at 1 g with a fusion torch. You do not have to set anything up to see an answer.

  1. Look at The answer panel on the right. It already shows how long the trip takes for you and for Earth.
  2. Press Fly this flight in the bottom panel. A ship marker crosses the map while the clocks run.
  3. Try a famous flight from the row of buttons in the bottom panel, such as Apollo to the Moon, Sol to Proxima Centauri or 1 g to Andromeda.
  4. Make it your own: change From and To in the Flight plan panel on the left, click a different engine card, or drag a slider.
  5. Press Share this flight in the top bar to copy a link to exactly what you built.

Choosing Places

The Flight plan panel has two pickers: From (where you leave) and To (where you arrive). The distance between them is the straight-line distance in space.

Searching

Click a picker and type. Search covers both maps at once, so you can find anything without switching first. It is forgiving:

  • Nicknames and aliases — "Alpha Centauri", "North Star", "M31", "galactic center".
  • Expanded names — "Epsilon Eridani".
  • Constellations — type "orion" to list its stars.
  • Kinds of object — type "black hole" or similar.
  • Small typos — a near miss still finds the right object.

Use the ↑ and ↓ arrow keys to move through results and Enter to choose one. Hovering a result pings that object on the map, and its show on map button flies the camera there.

Browsing

If you would rather not type, open the picker and use the browse lists for ready-made groups of places such as planets, nearby stars and famous objects.

Swapping and switching maps

The swap button between the pickers reverses the trip. If you choose a place that lives on the other map (a star while you are looking at the Solar system, say), the app switches maps for you.

Choosing an Engine

Five engine cards sit under the pickers. Each card shows how long this trip would take with that engine, drawn as a bar on one shared logarithmic scale. Every bar follows the same ruler, so you can compare a chemical rocket with a warp drive at a glance even when one takes days and the other takes millennia. Click a card to pick that engine.

Each sublight engine remembers its own acceleration and speed-limit settings while you try the others.

Tuning the Ship

Below the engine cards are sliders for the selected engine. The Reset button returns them to the engine's defaults.

Acceleration (sublight engines)

How hard the engines push, in multiples of Earth's surface gravity (1 g = 9.80665 m/s²). It is the push the crew feel, so 1 g feels like standing on Earth. The slider is logarithmic because ranges span up to a factor of 300, and a short plain-language note tells you what the value would feel like.

Speed limit (sublight engines)

The fastest the ship is allowed to go. Slow engines show it in km/s; fast ones show a fraction of the speed of light (c). For the antimatter rocket the slider is spaced by "nines" (0.9 c, 0.99 c, 0.999 c) so the relativistic end is easy to reach.

Coasting: If the ship hits its speed limit before the halfway point, it stops accelerating and coasts at constant speed, then performs a mirror-image braking burn so it arrives at rest. If the limit is never reached, the ship flips at the midpoint and brakes straight away.

Warp factor (warp drive)

For the warp drive the speed-limit slider is replaced by a warp factor from 1× to 50×. A factor of 10 means ten light-years covered per year on Earth.

Ship mass

How heavy the ship is. It does not change the travel time; it changes the energy the trip needs, shown in "In perspective". The slider is logarithmic, and a ship class label goes with the value:

The Answer Panel

The right-hand panel turns the numbers into something you can feel.

Two clocks

A You age clock shows how much time passes for the crew. An Earth ages clock shows how much passes for everyone back home. Each has a bar, so the gap is visible. For slow trips the two are almost equal; for fast ones they drift far apart. Under the clocks, one plain sentence states the difference in words.

Three stat tiles

TileMeaning
Peak speedThe fastest the ship goes, in km/s or as a fraction of light speed.
Lorentz γThe time-dilation factor at peak speed. One year on the ship equals γ years on Earth at that moment. It is always 1 for warp.
DistanceThe straight-line distance, in the unit that reads best (km, AU or light-years).

In perspective

Four comparisons put the trip in context:

  • Light travel time — how long a beam of light takes to cover the same distance.
  • A real mission — a comparison with an actual spacecraft, or with Voyager 1 for longer trips.
  • Human scale — what the duration means in human terms, such as lifetimes.
  • Energy — the trip's energy compared with how much energy the whole world uses.

The energy figure for a warp drive is a notional placeholder, not real physics. A real Alcubierre bubble would need exotic matter with negative energy density, and no one knows how much.

Playback and the Chart

The bottom panel lets you watch the flight rather than just read about it.

  • Famous flights — a row of one-click flights, from Apollo to the Moon to 1 g to Andromeda.
  • Play / pause — also bound to the Space bar.
  • Speed — playback runs from 0.5× to 4×.

The speed chart

The chart plots speed against time. A sublight flight rises, may level off if it reaches its speed limit (the coast), and falls again for the braking burn. A warp flight is a flat line, because the bubble moves at one steady speed.

The chart is also the scrubber: click or drag on it to jump to any moment in the flight. With the chart focused, the ← and → arrow keys step through the flight.

Even time or Stretch the burns

The toggle under the chart changes the time axis.

  • Even time — every moment of the trip gets the same width. This is honest, but a burn of a few days vanishes inside a trip of decades.
  • Stretch the burns — uses a logarithmic time axis so the short acceleration and braking burns stay visible next to a very long coast.

During playback

A ship marker travels along the path. On sublight flights a flip marker shows the point where the ship turns around to brake.

Layers

The Layers menu in the top bar switches map details on and off:

  • Object names — labels next to objects.
  • Exoplanets — planets known to orbit other stars.
  • Orbits — the orbit lines of solar-system bodies.
  • Milky Way backdrop — the panorama behind the stars.

Sharing a Flight

Press Share this flight in the top bar. The app copies a link and briefly shows Link copied. The whole flight (places, engine and settings) lives in the page address, so anyone who opens the link sees exactly the same plan. You can also bookmark the address or copy it from the browser's address bar.

Phones and Tablets

On a phone or tablet the map fills the screen and the panels move into a bottom sheet. Drag the handle at the top of the sheet up for more room or down to see more map. Three tabs switch what the sheet shows:

  • Plan — pickers, engines and sliders.
  • Answer — the clocks, stat tiles and comparisons.
  • Flight — playback, readouts and the speed chart.

Pinch to zoom the map and drag with two fingers to pan.

Accessibility

Controls can be reached and used with the keyboard, and the main controls carry text labels for screen readers. If your device is set to reduce motion, the app honours that setting and tones down animation. The chart scrubber has arrow-key control, so playback position never depends on a mouse.

Relativistic Travel Explained

At high speed, time passes more slowly for a moving observer than for a stationary one. This time dilation is governed by the Lorentz factor:

γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)

The engine uses constant-proper-acceleration equations. "Proper acceleration" is the acceleration the crew feel, the kind a scale on board would measure. For an acceleration a over a distance x (starting from rest):

  • Earth time: t = (c/a) × √( (1 + ax/c²)² − 1 )
  • Ship time: τ = (c/a) × acosh(1 + ax/c²)

A real trip has two halves: accelerate, then brake. The engine computes one half and mirrors it. If the speed limit is reached first, a coasting stretch is added in the middle.

Worked Example: Two Clocks Drift Apart

Choose Sol to Proxima Centauri (4.2465 light-years), the antimatter rocket, and 1 g. This is the "Sol to Proxima Centauri" famous flight.

  • The ship never reaches the 0.999 c speed limit. It speeds up for the first half, flips at the midpoint at about 0.95 c (γ about 3.2), and brakes for the second half.
  • About 5.9 years pass on Earth.
  • About 3.5 years pass on the ship.

Now stretch the distance. At 1 g to the Andromeda galaxy with the 0.999 c speed limit, the ship spends most of the trip coasting at the limit (γ about 22.4). That takes about 2.54 million years on Earth but only about 114,000 years on the ship. Run it yourself with the "1 g to Andromeda" famous flight and watch the two clocks.

Time dilation is not about the ship feeling anything odd. Aboard, life goes on normally at 1 g. The difference only shows when the two clocks are compared at the end.

Warp Drive Physics

The Alcubierre warp metric (1994) is a theoretical solution to Einstein's equations. The ship does not outrun light through space. Instead, space itself contracts ahead of the ship and expands behind it. Inside the bubble the ship sits in flat space-time:

  • No felt acceleration — the crew float weightlessly.
  • Lorentz factor = 1 — no time dilation. Ship time and Earth time are equal.
  • Apparent speed is the warp factor times c, in a straight line (a linear model).

A real Alcubierre bubble needs exotic matter with negative energy density, which is not known to exist. The warp energy shown in the app is a notional placeholder built on an arbitrary reactor constant. It is not derived from physics.

Data Catalogs

Solar System

Generated from the JPL Horizons ephemeris service. Each body has its real 3D position in AU, centred on the Sun, and an orbit path for drawing. The set includes the Sun, the eight planets, the Moon, dwarf planets, asteroids and spacecraft.

Stars and beyond

A merged dataset from three sources:

  1. HYG Database v3.6 — stars from the Henry Draper, Yale Bright Star and Hipparcos catalogs, filtered to named stars and bright ones. 3D positions in parsecs relative to the Sun.
  2. NASA Exoplanet Archive — confirmed exoplanets within 100 parsecs, retrieved through the archive's TAP service.
  3. Curated anchors — Messier objects (nebulae, clusters, galaxies), Sagittarius A*, and spacecraft such as Voyager 1 and 2, with short descriptions.

Glossary

TermDefinition
Proper Time (τ)Time as experienced by the traveller aboard the ship (the "You age" clock). At relativistic speeds it is shorter than Earth time.
Coordinate Time (t)Time measured by a stationary observer, such as someone on Earth (the "Earth ages" clock).
Lorentz Factor (γ)The ratio of Earth time to ship time at a given speed. At 0.99 c, γ is about 7.09. At 0.999 c, γ is about 22.4.
Proper AccelerationThe acceleration felt inside the ship, what a scale on board would read.
Flip and brakeThe standard deep-space manoeuvre: accelerate, turn the ship around, then decelerate to arrive at rest.
CoastA stretch at constant speed in the middle of the flight, used when the ship reaches its speed limit before the halfway point.
Alcubierre MetricA solution to Einstein's equations describing a "warp bubble" that allows apparent faster-than-light travel without breaking relativity locally.
AUAstronomical Unit, the average Earth–Sun distance. 1 AU is about 149.6 million km.
Parsec (pc)3.26 light-years, or 3.086 × 10¹⁶ metres. A standard unit of stellar distance.
Light-year (ly)The distance light travels in one year, about 9.461 × 10¹⁵ metres.
